\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Constellation.} Our main testbed is a Starlink Shell-1 Walker
constellation~\cite{giuliari2020} interconnected in a $+$Grid pattern
(\cref{tab:constellation}); orbital dynamics are derived from the
Satellite Tool Kit (STK). With 1{,}584 satellites, an average path length
of 23.51 hops, and a diameter of 47, capacity conflicts between demands
are far more frequent than in the terrestrial WANs used in prior TE
studies~\cite{teal}. For the cross-scale check
(\cref{sec:cross-scale}) we use a one-third-scale instance of the same
architecture (24 planes $\times$ 22 satellites, 528 nodes).

\textbf{Traffic.} Traffic matrices are generated by random city
sampling~\cite{giuliari2021}: cities are drawn with probability
proportional to their population, each sampled city pair is attached to
its nearest satellites, and per-pair flows are aggregated into
satellite-level demands (\cref{tab:tm-stats}). We use 101 consecutive
snapshots (one every 5 minutes), with snapshots 0--79 for training,
80--89 for validation, and 90--100 for testing, a strict temporal
split with no leakage. ISL capacity is calibrated so that the
fully-observed optimum leaves moderate congestion, keeping TE decisions
non-trivial. Demand pruning (\cref{sec:scaling}) restricts the modeled
demand set to the ${\sim}6.3$K pairs that ever carry traffic, out of
2.5M node pairs.

\textbf{Metric and protocol.} Our metric is the \emph{realized MLU}
evaluated against ground-truth demands (lower is better); flow
conservation in \cref{eq:conserve} is enforced by construction, so MLU
cannot be gamed by under-serving traffic. We sweep the observation
ratio $\rho \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$ and run three random
seeds per configuration. Unless stated otherwise, every method is
paired with the \emph{identical deployable repair layer}: two
rebalancing sweeps whose utilization estimate reads only the sparse
observation with neighbor fill (\textsc{obs-nbr}, \cref{sec:training}),
so that no method receives information a sparsely measuring controller
would not have, and the only factor that varies across methods is the
allocator. The \textsc{oracle} repair variant, which reads the
ground-truth matrix as inherited implementations do, appears only in
the analysis of \cref{sec:repair-design}. Because seed-to-seed
variation is comparable to between-configuration differences, headline
comparisons are \emph{paired} on $(\rho, \text{seed})$ and assessed
with two-sided sign tests; we report win counts and medians rather
than pooled means.

\textbf{Baselines.} All learned baselines share the training pipeline,
path sets, and repair layer of \sysname{} and differ only in how they
handle sparsity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{zero-fill}: the sparse adaptation of full-observation
    systems such as Teal~\cite{teal} and SaTE~\cite{sate}, which
    cannot run on an incomplete matrix; unobserved entries are set to
    zero and the model is trained end to end on the filled input.
    SaTE's heterogeneous-graph allocator targets the same
    MLU objective with the same pruning, so this variant is its
    closest sparse-input proxy.
    \item \emph{mean-interp}: a two-stage complete-then-optimize
    baseline that fills unobserved entries with the mean of observed
    demands before allocation.
    \item \emph{nbr-fill}: a stronger two-stage baseline that fills
    each unobserved demand from observed demands sharing its source
    satellite, the same estimate \sysname{} hands to repair.
    \item \emph{TEST-style}: a faithful re-implementation of
    TEST~\cite{test} in our pipeline: zero-filled input, a Transformer
    over the observation history ($L=3$), and an ungated GCN.
    \item \emph{untrained}: the \sysname{} architecture with weights
    frozen at random initialization, isolating the contribution of
    training.
    \item \emph{full observation}: \sysname{} given the complete
    matrix ($\rho=1$), an upper reference on input information.
    \item \emph{LP oracle}: the per-snapshot optimum of
    \crefrange{eq:mlu}{eq:mlu-nonneg} solved on ground-truth demands,
    a lower bound no online method can beat.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Learning and Repair Are Complementary}
\label{sec:learning-repair}

The overall result decomposes into two additive contributions, which
we isolate by switching each component off.

\textbf{Learning contributes on top of repair.} Comparing \sysname{}
with the untrained control in \cref{tab:overall}, both behind the
identical deployable repair, isolates what training the sparse-aware
allocator adds once repair is already correcting overloads:
\todo{paired wins and median gain per $\rho$ from overall\_main.csv}.
The gain is largest where the observation is too sparse for the
repair estimate to be accurate, which is exactly the regime the
learned prior is trained for.

\textbf{Learning is substantial on its own.} With the repair layer
switched off entirely, training improves the realized MLU at every
observability level tested: 13 of 15 paired cells across
$\rho \in [0.02, 0.5]$ (two-sided sign test $p=0.018$), with a median
gain peaking at $+28.1\%$ at $\rho=0.3$ and remaining at
$+16.5\%$ even at $\rho=0.02$ (\cref{fig:rq2}). There is no
observability cutoff below which the learned signal collapses: the
mask embeddings continue to regularize allocations when only $2\%$ of
demands are observed.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/rq2.pdf}
    \caption{Training gain without repair as a function of the
    observation ratio. Learning improves over the untrained control at
    every $\rho$ tested; the gain is non-monotone but never collapses.}
    \label{fig:rq2}

\textbf{Repair contributes what learning cannot.} Conversely, the
deployable repair corrects the residual overloads that any
finite-capacity policy leaves: on the untrained control at
$\rho=0.3$ it cuts the median MLU from $2.833$ to $1.740$
(\cref{tab:ladder}). The two components address different errors,
allocation quality versus feasibility residuals, and neither
subsumes the other under the deployable protocol: removing either one
degrades the end-to-end MLU

\subsection{Designing the Repair Information Source}
\label{sec:repair-design}

The deployable repair layer is itself a design contribution: its
effectiveness depends critically on \emph{which} traffic estimate its
rebalancing sweeps read, a degree of freedom that full-observation
systems never had to consider.

\textbf{The information ladder.} \Cref{tab:ladder} evaluates the same
rebalancing algorithm fed with three different matrices, on the
untrained control at $\rho=0.3$ so that no learning is involved and
only the information given to repair varies. The ordering is
unambiguous: zero-filled repair recovers only a fraction of the
possible improvement (median MLU $2.295$ from $2.833$), because zeros
hide the load that unmeasured traffic places on links and the sweeps
misrank exactly the paths that matter. The neighbor estimate closes
most of the gap to the non-deployable oracle ($1.740$ vs.\ $1.599$),
confirming that a per-source completion carries enough signal to rank
congested paths nearly as well as the ground truth. The answer to
``how should repair be designed under sparse observation'' is
therefore concrete: keep the rebalancing algorithm, but feed it the
best observation-derived estimate available; the choice of that
estimate is worth more than additional sweeps.

\textbf{Why the oracle variant is excluded from the main comparison.}
Beyond being undeployable, oracle-fed repair distorts method
comparisons: because it already knows every demand volume, a few
sweeps drive very different allocations toward the same equilibrium
and flatten the differences between allocators.
\Cref{tab:saturation} makes this concrete by sweeping the repair
budget at $\rho=0.3$ under oracle repair: a single budget unit
collapses the $+28.1\%$ training gain to $-3.3\%$, and further sweeps
change nothing. Under the same oracle repair, the model given the
complete matrix is even indistinguishable from sparse models (median
MLU $1.692$ at $\rho=1$ vs.\ $1.587$--$1.665$ at $\rho \le 0.5$),
although more observation can never truly hurt. An evaluation
protocol that grants repair the ground truth therefore cannot
certify the value of any sparse-input design, which is why our main
comparison in \cref{sec:overall} restricts every method to
observation-fed repair.

\subsection{Stability across Seeds}
\label{sec:stability}

Beyond the median, a deployable controller cares about the tails:
telemetry degradation must not occasionally produce a catastrophic
allocation. \Cref{tab:stability} and \cref{fig:stability} compare the
seed spread and the worst-case MLU of \sysname{} against zero-fill per
$\rho$ (5 seeds, repair enabled): \sysname{} has the smaller spread at
all five observability levels and the better worst case at four of
five, with the margin widening as sparsity grows, up to $+38.0\%$
at $\rho=0.02$, where zero-filling's worst seed degrades to $2.734$
while \sysname{} stays at $1.694$. The learned mask embeddings act as
a calibrated prior that protects against unlucky demand-seed
combinations, precisely the property a controller needs when the
metered subset is small.

\subsection{Cross-Scale Generality}
\label{sec:cross-scale}

To test whether the results depend on constellation scale, we
replicate the decisive cells on a one-third-scale instance of the same
Shell-1 architecture (528 satellites, 2{,}112 ISLs, 93 traffic
snapshots, demand density ${\sim}10\times$ higher per node pair),
reusing the weights-shared architecture without any structural change.
\Cref{tab:cross-scale} reports the trained/untrained pairing with and
without repair at $\rho \in \{0.3, 0.05\}$. The qualitative picture
carries over: learning contributes strongly in the repair-free regime
($+24.4\%$ at $\rho=0.05$, 3/3 paired wins), and the repair layer
delivers a large absolute improvement on top. The observability level
at which learning helps most shifts with the heavier per-node load
(the repair-free peak moves from $\rho=0.3$ on the main constellation
to $\rho=0.05$ here), which is expected: what constitutes ``enough
observation to learn from'' depends on traffic density, not on the
mechanism itself.

\subsection{Inference Latency}
\label{sec:runtime}

On the 1{,}584-satellite constellation, one full inference pass
(demand-set assembly, graph rebuild, gated GNN, temporal encoder,
fusion, policy head, and the two-sweep repair) completes in under
$0.4$~s per snapshot on a single GPU, against a control-loop period of
5 minutes. The repair step is structurally cheap: two sweeps amount to
twenty sparse scatter operations over the path-link incidence, so the
observation-fed redesign of \cref{sec:repair-design} costs nothing in
latency relative to the inherited implementation. Combined with the
$O(D)$ complexity of \cref{sec:complexity} and the 4{,}468-parameter
model size, \sysname{} comfortably fits the compute envelope of a
constellation controller.

\subsection{Takeaways}
\label{sec:takeaways}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Under sparse observation with a deployable,
    observation-fed repair layer, \sysname{} delivers the lowest
    realized MLU across the entire observability range tested, and
    the advantage grows as observation becomes scarcer.
    \item Learning and repair are complementary: the learned
    sparse-aware allocator improves the allocation prior, the
    observation-fed repair corrects residual overloads, and removing
    either degrades end-to-end performance.
    \item How repair is designed matters as much as whether it is
    present: feeding the rebalancing a neighbor-based estimate
    recovers most of the (undeployable) oracle's benefit, whereas
    zero-filling forfeits half of it. Evaluations that let repair
    read the ground truth flatten method differences and should be
    avoided when comparing sparse-input TE systems.
\end{itemize}
