\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Constellation.} Our testbed is a Starlink-like Walker
constellation interconnected in a $+$Grid pattern, with parameters listed
in \cref{tab:constellation}; the orbital dynamics are derived from the
Satellite Tool Kit (STK). \Cref{tab:topos} compares its graph
characteristics with the terrestrial topologies used in prior TE
studies~\cite{teal}, which we additionally employ for generalization
experiments: the constellation combines a large node count with a long
average path length (23.51 hops) and diameter (47), making capacity
conflicts between demands substantially more frequent than in
terrestrial WANs.

\textbf{Traffic.} Traffic matrices are generated by random city
sampling~\cite{giuliari2021}: cities are drawn with probability
proportional to their population (or GDP), each sampled city pair is
attached to its nearest satellites, and per-pair flows are aggregated
into satellite-level demands. We use the population-based trace as the
default: 101 consecutive snapshots (one every 5 minutes) that capture
real geographic demand distribution and temporal drift (${\sim}10\%$
demand-pair turnover across the trace); the GDP-based variant, whose
traffic is thinner but spread over ${\sim}20\times$ more demand pairs
(\cref{tab:tm-stats}), is used in the sensitivity study. ISL
capacity is calibrated so that the fully-observed optimum leaves moderate
congestion (satisfied ratio ${\approx}0.87$), keeping TE decisions
non-trivial. Snapshots 0--79 are used for training, 80--89 for
validation, and 90--100 for testing --- a strict temporal split with no
leakage. \Cref{tab:tm-stats} summarizes the trace;
\cref{fig:traffic-char} illustrates its two properties that
\sysname{} exploits: strong short-term temporal correlation of
per-satellite volumes, and pronounced geographic concentration ---
satellites over populated regions carry orders of magnitude more traffic
than those over oceans, which is what makes demand pruning
(\cref{sec:scaling}) effective.

\textbf{Metrics and baselines.} Our primary metric is the
\emph{realized MLU}, evaluated against ground-truth demands. Following
TEST~\cite{test}, we report it as the normalized performance ratio
$PR = U / U_{\mathrm{opt}}$, where $U_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is the optimal MLU
obtained by solving the LP~\eqref{eq:mlu}--\eqref{eq:mlu-nonneg} on the
true traffic matrix ($PR \ge 1$, lower is better); MLU alone can be gamed
by under-serving traffic, which our flow-conservation
formulation~\eqref{eq:conserve} rules out by construction. As the
secondary objective we report the \emph{satisfied demand ratio}
(allocated flow after ADMM and rounding over total demand). Both metrics
and per-snapshot inference latency are averaged over three seeds
(mean~$\pm$~std). Following the
taxonomy of \cref{sec:paradigm}, we compare against \emph{Zero-fill}
(unobserved entries set to zero, no mask-aware modules), \emph{Mean-interp.}
(two-stage complete-then-optimize), and \emph{Teal-full} (original Teal with
the complete matrix, an upper reference).

\subsection{Zero-Retraining under Demand and Topology Drift}
\label{sec:zero-retrain}

\textbf{Demand-set drift.} We train on the demand-pair union of the
training slice (6,320 pairs) and,
at test time, rebuild the graph from the test slice's own union (6,044
pairs, including pairs never seen in training) while keeping the weights
frozen. \Cref{tab:split} shows that this incurs negligible loss relative to
the oracle setting where the demand set is built from all snapshots, and
\cref{fig:exp-split} confirms the gap stays small on every test snapshot,
validating train-once, zero-retraining deployment under demand-set drift.

\textbf{Topology drift.} Constellation topologies also change --- ISLs
fail or are re-planned as satellites move. We emulate this by testing the
model, trained on the nominal $+$Grid topology, on perturbed variants with
$5\%$/$10\%$ of ISLs removed (candidate paths and the bipartite graph are
rebuilt at test time; weights stay frozen), and additionally with $k$
random link failures injected at test time. As \cref{tab:topo-drift}
shows, \sysname{} degrades marginally under both perturbations,
substantiating Proposition~\ref{prop:invariance}: the graph is a runtime
input, not a parameter.

\subsection{Runtime and Scalability}
\label{sec:runtime}

Demand pruning shrinks the modeled problem from 2.5M pairs to ${\sim}6.3$K
(${\sim}400\times$), bringing per-snapshot inference to \todo{XX}~ms on a
single GPU --- within the control budget of a rapidly changing
constellation, and comparable to the millisecond-scale latency reported by
SaTE~\cite{sate}. \Cref{fig:exp-runtime} contrasts \sysname{} with an LP
solver and Teal-full: learned inference is three orders of magnitude faster
than solving, and the temporal module adds only marginal latency. Modeling
the full demand set without pruning is infeasible under the memory budget,
as the index tensors alone would exceed \todo{XX}~GB.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/exp-runtime.pdf}
    \caption{Per-snapshot inference latency (log scale, placeholder data).}
    \label{fig:exp-runtime}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Control-loop budget.} Inference is only part of the control loop:
collecting sparse measurements and disseminating split ratios traverse
ground--satellite and inter-satellite links, adding propagation delays on
the order of a few tens of milliseconds in LEO. Millisecond-scale
inference keeps the end-to-end loop dominated by propagation rather than
computation; moreover, since the trained model has fewer than 5{,}000
parameters (\cref{sec:complexity}), it can also be replicated onboard to
shorten the dissemination path.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\textbf{Measurement granularity.} \Cref{fig:exp-obstype} compares flow- and
node-level sampling at equal $\rho$. Node-level missing is structural ---
entire source rows disappear (\cref{fig:sparse-tm}) --- and hurts the
zero-fill baseline substantially more, while \sysname{} remains robust
thanks to spatial message passing from links shaped by observed traffic.

\textbf{History length.} \Cref{fig:exp-histlen} sweeps $L$: quality
saturates around $L{=}\todo{X}$ while latency grows linearly, so we adopt
$L{=}3$ by default.

\textbf{Load sensitivity.} \Cref{fig:exp-capacity} varies ISL capacity:
the advantage of \sysname{} widens under high load, where misallocating
unmeasured demands is most costly.

\textbf{Traffic model.} On the GDP-based trace --- thinner flows spread
over ${\sim}20\times$ more demand pairs (\cref{tab:tm-stats}) --- the
pruned demand set grows to ${\sim}126$K pairs, stressing the scalability
of the pipeline; \sysname{} retains \todo{XX}\% of the fully-observed
performance at $\rho=0.3$, showing that the sparse-aware design does not
depend on a particular traffic concentration pattern.
