\subsection{Setup and Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Constellation.} Our main testbed is a Starlink Shell-1 Walker
constellation~\cite{giuliari2020} interconnected in a $+$Grid pattern
(\cref{tab:constellation}); orbital dynamics are derived from the
Satellite Tool Kit (STK). With 1{,}584 satellites, an average path length
of 23.51 hops, and a diameter of 47, capacity conflicts between demands
are far more frequent than in the terrestrial WANs used in prior TE
studies~\cite{teal}. For the cross-scale check
(\cref{sec:cross-scale}) we use a one-third-scale instance of the same
architecture (24 planes $\times$ 22 satellites, 528 nodes).

\textbf{Traffic.} Traffic matrices are generated by random city
sampling~\cite{giuliari2021}: cities are drawn with probability
proportional to their population, each sampled city pair is attached to
its nearest satellites, and per-pair flows are aggregated into
satellite-level demands (\cref{tab:tm-stats}). We use 101 consecutive
snapshots (one every 5 minutes), with snapshots 0--79 for training,
80--89 for validation, and 90--100 for testing --- a strict temporal
split with no leakage. ISL capacity is calibrated so that the
fully-observed optimum leaves moderate congestion, keeping TE decisions
non-trivial. Demand pruning (\cref{sec:scaling}) restricts the modeled
demand set to the ${\sim}6.3$K pairs that ever carry traffic, out of
2.5M node pairs.

\textbf{Protocol.} Our metric is the \emph{realized MLU} evaluated
against ground-truth demands (lower is better); flow
conservation~\eqref{eq:conserve} is enforced by construction, so MLU
cannot be gamed by under-serving traffic. The study varies five factors
independently: observability
$\rho \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$; the repair budget
$\in \{0, 1, 2, 5\}$ sweeps ($2$ is the deployed default); the matrix
the repair step is allowed to read (\cref{sec:training}) --- the
ground-truth one (\textsc{oracle}, the inherited default) or the sparse
observation (\textsc{obs-zero}, \textsc{obs-nbr}); training status
(trained vs.\ an \emph{untrained} control sharing the identical
architecture, mask embeddings, and repair layer, but with weights frozen
at random initialization); and five random seeds. Unless stated
otherwise, ``repair on'' means the \textsc{oracle} setting, so that
\cref{sec:rq1,sec:rq2,sec:rq3} reproduce the configuration a Teal-style
system would inherit; \cref{sec:deployable} then removes that
assumption. Because seed-to-seed
variation turned out to be comparable to between-configuration
differences, all headline comparisons are \emph{paired} on
$(\rho, \text{seed})$ and assessed with two-sided sign tests; we report
win counts and medians rather than pooled means. Throughout, the
\emph{training gain} of a configuration is the median over seeds of the
per-seed relative difference
$(\mathrm{MLU}_{\mathrm{untrained}} - \mathrm{MLU}_{\mathrm{trained}})
/ \mathrm{MLU}_{\mathrm{untrained}}$, so positive values mean training
helps. This paired statistic and the difference of the two median MLUs
need not agree --- not even in sign --- when seed-level rankings
disagree; we therefore treat the paired statistic as authoritative and
report medians only for reference.

\subsection{RQ1: Does Learning Contribute When Repair Is Present?}
\label{sec:rq1}

No. \Cref{fig:rq1} compares the trained system against the untrained
control across all five observability levels, both with the default
two-sweep repair, and \cref{tab:rq1-strat} gives the paired statistics
per $\rho$. The trained system wins only 9 of 25 paired cells, with a
median paired gain of $-1.8\%$ --- marginally in favour of the
untrained control --- and a sign test does not reject indifference
($p=0.115$ in the untrained direction; the trained direction is far
from significance).

A non-significant test alone cannot establish the absence of an effect,
so we bound the effect size directly. Across the 25 paired cells the
mean paired gain is $-2.49\%$ with a standard deviation of $7.38$
points, giving a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[-5.54\%, +0.56\%]$.
The upper end is the informative one: we can \emph{rule out} any
training-induced improvement larger than $0.6\%$. This is a positive
statement of absence rather than a failure to detect --- whatever the
network learns, repair leaves at most half a percent of it visible at
the output. The pipeline output is essentially determined before the
learned weights are consulted.

The per-$\rho$ breakdown adds a structured detail we report for
completeness: the sign of the effect is not uniform. At the sparsest
setting ($\rho=0.02$) training is mildly beneficial ($+4.8\%$, 3/5
wins), whereas from $\rho=0.1$ upward the untrained control is
consistently ahead, taking 3/5, 4/5, and 4/5 cells as $\rho$ grows. We
do not claim that training is \emph{harmful} --- the interval above
comfortably includes zero --- but the absence of any regime in which
repair-backed training clearly helps is itself the finding.

\textbf{Repair acts as a switch, not a dial.} To establish that repair
is what erases the learned signal --- rather than merely coinciding with
its absence --- we sweep the repair budget from zero upward with
everything else held fixed. \Cref{fig:saturation} and
\cref{tab:saturation} report both the absolute medians and the paired
training gain at each budget, and the two tell opposite stories. In \emph{absolute} terms repair is enormously
valuable: it lowers the median MLU from $2.83$ to $1.60$, a $44\%$
improvement that no amount of training comes close to --- though
\cref{sec:deployable} will show that part of this figure is the
information repair reads rather than the rebalancing itself. In
\emph{differential} terms it is destructive: the training gain collapses
from $+28.1\%$ (3/3 paired wins) at zero budget to $-3.3\%$ (1/3) after
a single unit, and never recovers at two or five, whose medians
differ by less than the seed spread with no monotone trend. The
transition completes within the first unit --- there is no
intermediate budget at which learning and repair each contribute a
share.

Why should one sweep suffice? The rebalancing ranks each demand's paths
by the bottleneck utilization of the links they traverse and shifts flow
down that ranking (\cref{sec:training}). The ranking is dominated by
where the traffic actually is, not by the split ratios it started from,
so a handful of sweeps drives very different inputs --- learned or random
--- towards the same local equilibrium, and the differences the network
learned are rebalanced away. Repair thus \emph{absorbs} whatever the
network has learned, and the two components behave as functional
substitutes rather than complements.

There is a second, sharper reason, and it is the one
\cref{sec:deployable} pursues: to rank paths by utilization at all, the
repair step must know demand volumes. In the configuration measured
above --- the one a Teal-style system inherits --- it reads them from the
\emph{ground-truth} matrix. Repair therefore enters the comparison with
strictly more information than the policy, which sees only a fraction
$\rho$ of the same matrix. Under that asymmetry, it is not surprising
that learned imputation adds nothing: repair already knows what the
imputation was trying to guess.

\subsection{RQ2: When Does Learning Contribute Without Repair?}
\label{sec:rq2}

Removing the repair layer exposes the learned component's true
contribution (\cref{fig:rq2}). Training helps at \emph{every}
observability level we tested, winning 13 of 15 paired cells across
$\rho \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$ (two-sided sign test
$p=0.018$). The gain is non-monotone but never collapses: it
peaks at $+28.1\%$ (median) at $\rho=0.3$, dips to $+9.8\%$ at
$\rho=0.1$, and rebounds to $+16.4\%$ ($\rho=0.05$) and $+16.5\%$
($\rho=0.02$). Even at the sparsest setting we measure, two of three
seeds still improve over the untrained control.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/rq2.pdf}
    \caption{Training gain without repair as a function of
    observability. Learning improves over the untrained control at
    every $\rho$ tested; the gain is non-monotone but never collapses.}
    \label{fig:rq2}
\end{figure}

The absence of a collapse boundary is itself the finding. We expected,
from the gradient-diagnosis in \cref{sec:mask-embed}, that extreme
sparsity would starve the learned signal; instead the mask embeddings
continue to regularize allocations even when only 2\% of demands are
observed, and the remaining 98\% fill values do not destabilize the
softmax over path logits. What does vary is the gain shape: the midband
peak at $\rho=0.3$ reflects the regime where observations are
informative enough to steer allocations but not dense enough to make
learning redundant, while the rebound at $\rho \le 0.05$ reflects seed
variance in the untrained control (some random seeds produce
particularly bad allocations that training corrects).

\subsection{RQ3: What Does Learning Still Buy? Stability.}
\label{sec:rq3}

With repair present, the trained system's advantage over the standard
zero-fill baseline is real but modest in the median: 17 wins in
25 paired cells (one-sided $p=0.054$). The robust
advantage lies in the \emph{tails}. \Cref{tab:stability} and
\cref{fig:stability} report the seed spread and the worst-case MLU per
$\rho$: the trained system has the smaller spread at all five
observability levels and the better worst case at four of five, with the
margin widening as sparsity grows --- $+38.0\%$ at $\rho=0.02$, where
zero-filling's worst seed degrades to 2.734 while the trained system
stays at 1.694. Learned mask embeddings act as a calibrated prior that
protects against unlucky demand--seed combinations, which is precisely
the property a controller needs under telemetry degradation.

A subtler competitor is the neighbor-aware fill (\emph{nbr},
\cref{sec:mask-embed}): overall the trained system wins only
12/25 paired cells, but the wins and losses are
\emph{structured} --- \sysname{} dominates at $\rho \ge 0.3$
(4/5, 3/5) and loses at $\rho \le 0.1$. Local interpolation
is a strong prior when observations are few and spatially clustered;
learned embeddings earn their keep exactly when enough observations
exist to inform them. This crossover refines the window of
\cref{sec:rq2}: it is where learning beats not only no-learning but
also the best hand-crafted prior.

\subsection{The Full-Observation Paradox}
\label{sec:paradox}

A sanity check exposes how completely repair dominates the pipeline.
Information ordering demands that more observation never hurts, yet the
trained system at $\rho \le 0.5$ \emph{beats} the same model given the
complete traffic matrix at four of five $\rho$ values (median MLU
1.5865--1.6646 vs.\ 1.6918 at $\rho=1$). The paradox
dissolves once repair enters the picture: repair flattens the
performance landscape to the point where seed noise exceeds the
information effect, and sparse models win some seeds by luck. End-to-end
metrics under repair therefore cannot certify information value --- or
learning value --- at all.

This invites a fair objection: if repair flattens the landscape until
seed noise dominates, can any claim in this section be trusted? The
answer is that noise domination and our two positive claims are the same
observation seen from different sides. RQ1 asserts an \emph{absence} of
effect under repair, which is exactly what a noise-dominated landscape
produces --- and the confidence interval of \cref{sec:rq1} quantifies
how little room is left for one. RQ3 asserts an advantage in
\emph{spread and worst case}, quantities defined \emph{over} the seed
ensemble rather than contaminated by it. What noise domination does
invalidate is any claim of a median improvement under repair --- which
is precisely the claim we decline to make, and precisely the claim that
the conventional end-to-end protocol would have licensed.

\subsection{Cross-Scale Replication}
\label{sec:cross-scale}

To test whether absorption depends on constellation scale, we replicate
the decisive cells on a one-third-scale instance of the same Shell-1
architecture (528 satellites, 2{,}112 ISLs, 93 traffic snapshots,
demand density ${\sim}10\times$ higher per node pair).
\Cref{tab:cross-scale} and \cref{fig:crossscale} report the
trained/untrained pairing with and without repair at
$\rho \in \{0.3, 0.05\}$.

\textbf{Absorption replicates.} With repair enabled, training again
fails to establish a consistent direction: it takes 3 of 6 paired cells,
with a gain of $+5.7\%$ at $\rho=0.3$ (2/3 wins) and $-0.6\%$ at
$\rho=0.05$ (1/3) --- straddling zero exactly as on the main
constellation, with absolute medians differing by at most $0.06$.
Repair saturates and absorbs the learned signal at this scale too.

\textbf{The window shifts, not vanishes.} Without repair, the picture
diverges from the main topology. On the 1{,}584-satellite constellation,
training peaked at $\rho=0.3$ ($+28.1\%$) and stayed positive at
$\rho=0.05$ ($+16.4\%$). On the 528-node instance the peak moves to
$\rho=0.05$ ($+24.4\%$, 3/3 paired wins), while $\rho=0.3$ becomes
inconsistent ($-3.2\%$ paired median, 1/3 wins). This last cell is worth
a word of caution: its difference-of-medians has the \emph{opposite}
sign ($3.69$ vs $3.83$), because two seeds rank one way and the third,
with a large margin, the other; following the convention of
\cref{sec:setup} we report the paired value and treat the cell as
unresolved rather than as a win. The learning signal is still present
--- it is just repositioned by the heavier per-node load. This is the
stronger form of the core finding: absorption is \emph{global}
(invariant across scale), but the no-repair learning boundary is
\emph{local} (it shifts with traffic density).

\subsection{Does Absorption Reproduce on a Different Architecture?}
\label{sec:arch-transfer}

A natural worry is that absorption is a quirk of our particular model
rather than a property of the repair layer. We test this by re-running
the paired protocol on the closest prior sparse-input TE architecture,
TEST~\cite{test}: zero-filling for unobserved demands, a Transformer
over the observation history, and an ungated GCN over the topology
(\texttt{--mask-mode zero --no-gate --hist-len 3}). This is the
combination our ablations deliberately omit --- zero-fill plus
Transformer plus no mask-aware gate --- and it isolates the mask-aware
design as the only structural difference from \sysname{}.

\Cref{tab:arch-transfer} reports the four decisive cells. The contrast
with \cref{sec:rq1} is stark. Under repair at $\rho=0.3$, where
\sysname{} is fully absorbed (trained $\approx$ untrained, 9/25 paired
wins), the TEST-style architecture is \emph{not} absorbed: training
wins $2/3$ seeds with a $+8\%$ median gain. Without repair the two
architectures agree --- training helps strongly ($+17\%$, $3/3$) --- so
the divergence is specifically in how repair interacts with the learned
policy, not in whether learning helps at all.

\textbf{Absorption is architecture-specific.} On a mask-unaware
architecture the untrained control is unstable --- seed spread at
$\rho=0.3$ under repair spans $1.56$ to $1.89$, against the trained
control's tight $1.57$--$1.57$ --- so training retains value as a
stabiliser even when repair is on. On our mask-aware model the untrained
control is already competent (a random gate produces trivial imputation
that oracle repair handles well), so training and untraining converge
under repair. Absorption is therefore not a universal property of
constraint-repair layers; it arises where the learned component and
repair solve the same imputation subproblem, so repair subsumes the
policy.

\textbf{The gate drives absorption.} \Cref{tab:arch-ablation} isolates
the component. Holding the fill strategy fixed, turning the gate on
flips a non-absorbing cell ($2/3$ wins, $+2\%$ to $+8\%$) into an
absorbing one ($0/3$, $-2\%$ to $-14\%$); holding the gate fixed,
swapping the learnable placeholder for zero-fill changes the magnitude
but never the verdict. The mask-aware gate is what creates the overlap
with repair: with the gate, the policy's routing is
observation-conditioned, so oracle repair --- which also conditions on
the true demands --- subsumes it; without the gate, the policy routes
mask-agnostically and retains value repair cannot replicate. Absorption
is thus a property of mask-aware routing under oracle repair, not of
the repair layer or the placeholder alone.

\subsection{Inference Latency}
\label{sec:runtime}

Although latency is not the subject of this study, it determines whether
the mechanism we analyse is relevant to a deployable controller. On the
1{,}584-satellite constellation, one full inference pass --- demand-set
assembly, graph rebuild, gated GNN, temporal encoder, fusion, policy
head, and the two-sweep repair --- completes in under $0.4$~s per
snapshot on a single GPU, against a control-loop period of 5 minutes.
Repair is structurally cheap --- two sweeps amount to twenty sparse
scatter operations over the path--link incidence --- which is precisely
why it is attractive to keep, and why its interaction with learning
matters: inexpensive in latency, but, as \cref{sec:rq1} shows,
expensive in learned signal.

\subsection{Repair Without the Oracle}
\label{sec:deployable}

Every result so far granted the repair step the ground-truth traffic
matrix, because that is what the inherited implementation does
(\cref{sec:training}). Under sparse observation this is not a
reproducible deployment: a controller metering $\rho$ of its demands
cannot compute true link utilization. We therefore re-run the decisive
cells with repair restricted to what the controller can actually see ---
\textsc{obs-zero} and \textsc{obs-nbr} --- while the realized MLU is
still scored against the true demands, as everywhere else.

\textbf{Repair's advantage is largely informational.} \Cref{tab:ladder}
places the four repair settings on a common scale using the untrained
control at $\rho=0.3$, so that no learning is involved and only the
information available to repair varies. The ordering is unambiguous:
repair remains worth having when fed the observation --- neighbour-filled
repair still cuts median MLU from $2.83$ to $1.74$ --- but it is
markedly weaker than the oracle version at $1.60$, and zero-filled
repair is weaker still at $2.30$. A substantial part of what looked like the power
of \emph{repair} in \cref{sec:rq1} is in fact the power of the
\emph{ground-truth matrix} that repair was quietly reading.

\textbf{Does learning matter again?} Removing the oracle does not
cleanly restore learning's value. Under \textsc{obs-nbr} repair, the
trained policy wins $2/3$ seeds at $\rho{=}0.1$ ($+1\%$ median gain)
and $\rho{=}0.5$ ($+6\%$), yet loses at $\rho{=}0.3$ ($-8\%$,
$0/3$) and $\rho{=}0.05$ ($-9\%$, $1/3$). Under \textsc{obs-zero}
repair, training wins decisively at $\rho{=}0.05$ ($+18\%$, $3/3$)
but is flat at $\rho{=}0.3$ ($-1\%$, $1/3$). The absorption of
\cref{sec:rq1} is therefore \emph{partly} an oracle artifact---at
$\rho{=}0.1$ and $0.5$ learning helps once repair reads only the
observation---but it is not fully explained by the oracle: at
$\rho{=}0.3$, the densest point we test, training still does not help
even when repair is denied the ground truth. The contrast at
$\rho{=}0.05$ sharpens the point: under weak \textsc{obs-zero} repair
training helps ($+18\%$), yet under the stronger \textsc{obs-nbr}
repair it does not ($-9\%$) --- so it is the repair step's structural
rebalancing, not the oracle alone, that absorbs the policy's
contribution where repair is strong.

\subsection{Takeaways}
\label{sec:takeaways}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evaluation.} A learned TE system paired with a
    constraint-repair layer must be benchmarked repair-free or against
    repair-only controls; end-to-end numbers under repair conflate the
    two components and can certify a model whose weights contribute
    nothing. Report, in addition, \emph{which} traffic matrix the repair
    step is allowed to read: a repair layer holding the ground truth is
    not the same system as one holding the observation
    (\cref{sec:deployable}).
    \item \textbf{Design.} If repair is kept for safety, spend the
    learning budget on what repair cannot provide --- tail robustness
    (\cref{sec:rq3}) --- and evaluate it on spread and worst case, not
    medians.
    \item \textbf{Deployment.} Learning is effective across the
    entire observability range once repair is removed (13/15 paired
    wins), so there is no lower observability cutoff below which
    training should be abandoned. The operative choice is not whether to
    pay for repair but which matrix to hand it: the oracle-fed variant
    that erases the learned differential is precisely the variant a
    sparsely-measuring controller cannot run.
    \item \textbf{Scope of \cref{sec:rq1,sec:rq3}.} Those results
    characterize the oracle-fed configuration, which is what current
    systems inherit and report. The deployable variant
    (\cref{sec:deployable}) softens but does not erase absorption --- a
    residual remains at high $\rho$ --- so the oracle measurements are
    best read as an upper bound on absorption, not a deployable forecast.
    Conflating the two readings is how the effect stayed invisible.
\end{itemize}
