\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented a controlled mechanism study of learned traffic
engineering under sparse observations, using \sysname{} as the vehicle.
The central finding is \emph{repair absorption}: when a constraint-repair
layer is present, it acts as a switch rather than a dial --- a single
rebalancing sweep collapses the entire training differential, and
further sweeps change nothing --- leaving a trained network
indistinguishable from random initialization (9/25 paired wins on a
1{,}584-satellite Starlink constellation, with a $95\%$ interval on the
paired gain that rules out any improvement beyond $0.6\%$; 3/6 on a
one-third-scale instance). The absorption is global: it persists across
observability levels, random seeds, and constellation scales.

Without repair, learning is effective across the entire observability
range tested (13/15 paired wins, $\rho \in [0.02, 0.5]$), but the
gain profile is topology-dependent: the learning peak sits at
$\rho=0.3$ on the main constellation and shifts to $\rho=0.05$ on the
smaller, denser instance. The one robust residual advantage of the
trained system is stability --- lower seed spread and better worst-case
MLU (up to +38\% at extreme sparsity) --- not median performance.

The mechanism has a cause that the prevailing evaluation protocol keeps
out of view. To rank congested paths, repair needs demand volumes, and
the implementation inherited from fully-observed WAN settings takes them
from the ground-truth traffic matrix. Repair therefore competes with the
learned imputation while already knowing what that imputation was trying
to estimate --- an asymmetry that is unremarkable when the matrix is
known, and unavailable when it is not. Restricting repair to the sparse
observation costs it a substantial part of its advantage, which
separates two things the literature reports as one: the repair
\emph{algorithm} and the repair \emph{information}.

This distinction also fixes the scope of our results. The absorption
measurements characterize the oracle-fed configuration --- the one
current systems inherit and report --- so they are best read as a
diagnosis of an evaluation protocol rather than a forecast for a
deployable controller. Both readings are useful, and conflating them is
precisely how the effect stayed invisible.

The guidance follows directly. For evaluation, learned TE systems paired
with repair must be benchmarked repair-free or against repair-only
controls, and must state which matrix repair reads. For design, if
repair is retained for safety, the learning budget should target tail
robustness rather than median improvement. For deployment, training
remains worthwhile even at 2\% observability, and the operative question
is not whether to pay for repair but which matrix to hand it.

Three directions remain open. First, whether absorption survives when
the heuristic rebalancing is replaced by a genuine differentiable
projection --- dual-based projection or a feasibility layer in
differentiable optimization --- and whether such a layer, restricted to
the observation, absorbs less. Second, the stability advantage suggests
a dedicated training objective, explicitly optimizing worst-case MLU
rather than the mean, may unlock what the current loss leaves on the
table. Third, measurement scheduling --- deciding \emph{which} demands
to observe next --- becomes especially valuable once repair no longer has
free access to the full matrix, since each observation then improves the
allocator and the repair step at once.
